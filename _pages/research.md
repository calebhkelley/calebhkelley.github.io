I study how people use the sounds of speech to signal who they are, and how listeners read those signals. Small details that seem incidental, like how long a consonant lasts or where the pitch rises, turn out to be some of the main tools speakers use to project a persona and place themselves socially. My work asks which of these features carry social meaning, how they add up to a recognizable persona, and whether listeners from different backgrounds hear them the same way.

Put simply, I study how we hear personality and identity in a voice, and why two people can come away hearing the same voice differently.

Most of my research develops these questions through masculinity and sexual identity in Spanish. Andalusia has a well-known folk stereotype, the *pluma*, that gay men speak in a recognizably different way. I use that stereotype as a way into bigger questions about how speech signals gender, sexuality, and regional identity all at once. I work within third-wave sociolinguistics, which treats phonetic variation not as noise but as a resource speakers use to build social identities in real time.

## Current Projects

<div class="project-block" markdown="1">
### Masculinity, Persona, and the *Pluma* Stereotype in Sevillian Spanish
**Dissertation Research**

My dissertation investigates the *pluma* stereotype in Andalusian Spanish, the belief that gay men speak in a recognizably different way. Rather than simply comparing "gay speakers" to "straight speakers," I use a performance-based approach. Participants produce speech in different persona conditions (baseline, gay-sounding, straight-sounding), which lets me isolate the phonetic features speakers themselves associate with masculinity and sexual identity, rather than assuming those features in advance.

The key finding is that syllable-final /s/ duration and spectral center of gravity shift systematically across these conditions, and that /s/ works as an iconic marker of gay male speech, a pattern that turns out to hold across languages.
</div>

<div class="project-block" markdown="1">
### Perceiving Social Identity in Native and Non-Native Spanish
**Current IRB-Approved Study**

This study flips the question from production to perception. Can native and non-native Spanish speakers tell who "sounds gay" from short audio clips, and do they rely on the same phonetic cues?

Sixty participants (30 native, 30 non-native) complete two tasks, an identification task and a social trait attribution task, using stimuli from speakers performing baseline, gay, and straight persona conditions. The speaker pool includes Sevillian, Venezuelan, and Basque Spanish varieties, so I can also look at how regional dialect interacts with perceived identity.

This project sits at the intersection of sociophonetics, second language acquisition, and queer linguistics, an area that has barely been explored.
</div>

<div class="project-block" markdown="1">
### Prosody and Social Meaning Across LGBTQ+ and Straight Speakers
**In Progress**

This project turns to prosody, examining pitch accents and intonation patterns in Andalusian Spanish across speakers of different sexual orientations. I'm analyzing declaratives, exclamatives, and imperatives using Sp_ToBI notation to see whether prosodic features carry social meaning in the same way segmental ones do.
</div>

## Research Interests

Sociophonetics and phonetic variation · social meaning and speaker personae · language, gender, and masculinity · language and sexuality · speech perception and second language acquisition · Spanish intonation and prosody · performance-based methodology · LGBTQ+ linguistics

## Methods

My toolkit includes acoustic analysis in Praat, perception experiments (including matched-guise designs), statistical modeling of phonetic variation, and Sp_ToBI transcription for prosodic analysis. I prioritize performance-based methods that capture how speakers actively construct identity through speech, rather than treating social categories as fixed.
